# Methodological appraisal of a Chinese expert consensus on spinal cord stimulation for diabetic foot

**Running title**: Appraisal of an SCS-for-DF consensus

## Abstract

**Background**: Guidelines for spinal cord stimulation (SCS) in diabetic foot (DF) increasingly carry explicit evidence grades, yet development quality and the correspondence between recommendations and cited evidence are seldom appraised together. We appraised a 2026 Chinese expert consensus on SCS for DF with three complementary instruments and tested whether its self-assigned evidence levels were supported by the sources it cites.

**Methods**: In this cross-sectional methodological evaluation of one consensus, development and reporting quality were assessed with AGREE II by two independent appraisers, reporting completeness with RIGHT (22 topics/35 subitems) by one reviewer, and certainty for nine outcomes across six recommendations with a GRADE profile reconstructed from the consensus' own cited literature by two independent appraisers using masked templates; the global quality rating was one appraiser's judgement. Agreement used exact agreement, κ and ICC(2,1) (AGREE II), and final-certainty and per-domain agreement (GRADE). Cited-source claims were checked against full texts where obtainable.

**Results**: AGREE II domain scores ranged from 41.7% to 83.3% for appraiser 1 and 33.3% to 72.2% for appraiser 2; the three highest-scoring domains matched, but editorial independence ranked fourth and last respectively. Exact item agreement was 39.1% (9/23), κ=0.258, weighted κ=0.651, ICC(2,1)=0.853 (95%CI 0.663–0.941). RIGHT classified 15 subitems Yes, 16 Unclear, 4 No. In the GRADE reconstruction, eight of nine outcomes were very low certainty and one low; indirectness (DF versus painful diabetic neuropathy; limb salvage versus pain endpoints) was cross-cutting, and four of six recommendations had no usable evidence body. Two appraisers agreed on final certainty for all nine outcomes; per-domain agreement ranged from 44.4% (indirectness) to 100% (inconsistency), the latter reflecting uniformly 'not assessable' judgements. Four quantitative and one qualitative claim cited one reference containing neither SCS nor the claimed numbers.

**Conclusions**: The consensus reports scope and recommendations clearly but has substantial limitations in rigour, applicability and evidence linkage. Independent GRADE appraisers reached the same certainty conclusions through partly different downgrading paths. The findings support methodological revision rather than a judgement on SCS efficacy.

**Keywords**: clinical practice guideline; spinal cord stimulation; diabetic foot; AGREE II; RIGHT; GRADE; methodological appraisal

## 1 Introduction

Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) is used for refractory pain and, increasingly, for limb salvage in diabetic foot (DF) disease. In 2026 a Chinese expert consensus on SCS for DF was published [1] with explicit self-assigned evidence levels and recommendation strengths. Such labels communicate confidence to clinicians and can shape practice; their credibility depends both on the rigour of the consensus-development process and on whether the cited evidence actually supports the stated claims. When a guideline assigns a high evidence grade to a recommendation about limb salvage, the reader reasonably expects that the underlying studies enrolled patients with diabetic foot disease and measured limb-related outcomes. Whether that expectation is met is an empirical question about the document and its references, not a matter of clinical opinion.

The distinction between reporting quality, methodological rigour and evidence certainty is central to this appraisal. A guideline can describe its scope, users and recommendations with admirable clarity and still provide no account of how evidence was searched, selected or linked to each recommendation. Conversely, a low-certainty evidence base does not by itself make a recommendation wrong: grading frameworks explicitly allow strong recommendations in the face of low certainty when the alternatives are burdensome or ineffective. For these reasons we treated the three instruments as complementary rather than interchangeable, and reported each on its own terms instead of collapsing them into a single score or verdict.

Methodological appraisal of guidelines is well established. AGREE II (Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II) [2] evaluates reporting and development quality across six domains; RIGHT (Reporting Items for Practice Guidelines in Healthcare) [3] is a reporting checklist; GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) [4,5] rates certainty of evidence for each outcome separately from recommendation strength. These tools answer different questions and should not be substituted for one another: a guideline may report itself clearly while resting on weak evidence, and a strong recommendation may be appropriate despite low-certainty evidence when alternatives are poor. Applying all three to a single guideline, together with a source-level verification of the numbers cited, provides a more complete picture than any one instrument. The combination also makes the locus of any problem explicit: a RIGHT gap is a reporting omission, an AGREE II gap is a development or documentation weakness, and a GRADE downgrade is a statement about the evidence body for a defined outcome.

We therefore appraised the 2026 Chinese SCS-for-DF consensus with three objectives: (i) to quantify its reporting and methodological quality with AGREE II using two independent appraisers; (ii) to document reporting completeness with RIGHT and reconstruct certainty of evidence with GRADE from the consensus' own cited literature; and (iii) to verify, at full-text level, whether the numeric claims attached to the recommendations are supported by the sources they cite. We deliberately framed the study as an appraisal of a guideline, not as a systematic review of SCS efficacy, and did not pre-specify that the consensus was wrong. The appraisal was registered as part of a broader protocol on neuromodulation for limb salvage, and the present report concerns only this second-layer objective.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Study design and object

This was a cross-sectional methodological evaluation of one published clinical practice guideline. The object was the *Chinese expert consensus on spinal cord stimulation for diabetic foot (2026 edition)*, published in *Clinical Journal of Neurological Surgery* 2026;23(3):241–247, DOI 10.3969/j.issn.1672-7770.2026.03.001. The full typeset PDF was obtained and converted to normalised text (40,081 characters). Because the study evaluated a published document and its cited literature and involved no human participants, samples or identifiable data, institutional review board approval was not applicable.

### 2.2 Instruments and appraisal procedures

**AGREE II.** Two appraisers (Y.Y. and J.H.) scored all 23 items independently on the 1–7 scale. The second appraiser received a masked template containing only neutral section locators and the official item wording, with all first-appraiser scores and rationales removed; the anti-leak check found no judgement-language fingerprints. The second appraiser was instructed to read the normalised full text before scoring, to avoid consulting appraisal artefacts, and to record a short justification and a character locator for every item. Domain scores were normalised as (obtained − minimum)/(maximum − minimum) × 100. Domains were reported separately and were not summed into a single total, in accordance with the instrument. The overall quality rating and the recommendation to use were global judgements provided by appraiser 1 only — no second global rating was obtained and no agreement statistic is available for this component — and these are explicitly not derived from the domain scores. Two appraisers meet the instrument's stated minimum, though it recommends preferably four; this constraint is acknowledged in the interpretation of the agreement coefficients.

**RIGHT.** One reviewer (Y.Y.) applied the 22-topic/35-subitem RIGHT checklist, recording Yes / Unclear / No with a text locator for each subitem. RIGHT is a reporting guideline and not a quality-assessment instrument; we therefore used it only to document whether items were reported, and did not infer evidence quality from it.

**GRADE.** Two appraisers (Y.Y. and J.H.) independently constructed evidence profiles for nine outcomes across six recommendations. The second appraiser worked from a masked template containing only neutral PICO elements, cited-study descriptions and section locators, with all first-appraiser downgrading judgements, rationales and final certainties removed; an anti-leak check found no judgement-language fingerprints in the forwarded material. Starting certainty was High for randomised trials and Low for observational designs. Certainty was downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias, and upgraded for large effect, dose–response and confounding reduction, each decision being recorded with the underlying verified fact. Where only a single study was available, inconsistency was recorded as not assessable rather than as not serious; where no comparable body of evidence existed, the outcome was labelled very low certainty and the recommendation flagged as having no usable evidence body rather than being forced into a grade. The profiles were a retrospective reconstruction based solely on the literature cited by the consensus, not a de novo systematic search and synthesis; this boundary is a stated limitation. Because GRADE certainty is outcome-specific and distinct from recommendation strength, we rated each of the nine outcomes separately and recorded the consensus' self-assigned grade for comparison without treating it as an input. Domain judgements were normalised for wording before comparison, so that synonymous expressions of the same judgement (for example 'cannot assess' and 'not assessable') were not counted as disagreement.

### 2.3 Source verification and citation linkage

For each recommendation we mapped the adjacent reference numbers and recorded bibliographic and, where obtainable, full-text facts. Eight citations were prioritised for full-text verification; three OA aggregators and a user-supplied reference folder were cross-checked. Full text was verified for [6], which was checked against a full-text PDF supplied from the authors' document collection after all three open-access aggregators proved full-text closed, and for [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12] and [13]. Where a full text could not be obtained, the record was explicitly labelled as abstract-only or bibliographic-level and was not upgraded on inference. Head-text verification was used to match the user-supplied PDFs to reference numbers, and a corrected mapping was retained with the audit trail. For each of the five claims (four quantitative, one qualitative) in the consensus we recorded the cited source and tested the full text for the claimed number and for SCS-related terms. We recorded facts ("the cited source does not contain the number") without determining whether the claim was intentional or false; numerically similar values in other sources were logged only as candidate origins, not as verified sources, because their populations and endpoints differed from the claim.

### 2.4 Analysis and agreement

Descriptive statistics are counts and percentages. AGREE II agreement was assessed by exact item agreement, unweighted Cohen's κ, linearly weighted κ and ICC(2,1) (two-way, absolute agreement, single measure) with a 5,000-replicate bootstrap 95% CI. κ and ICC measure different aspects and both are reported. GRADE agreement was assessed primarily by exact agreement on final certainty across the nine outcomes and by the exact agreement rate for each of the five downgrading domains, together with an explicit list of every outcome-level disagreement with the domains involved. κ was computed but is reported as descriptive only: with nine outcomes and eight rated very low by both appraisers (expected agreement ≈0.80), κ=1.0 follows deterministically from perfect agreement in this near-constant distribution and conveys no discriminating information; a bootstrap interval for it degenerates to [1.00, 1.00]. We therefore did not use it as the headline result. Because the units were items or outcomes from a single guideline, all coefficients describe agreement for this document and do not generalise to other guidelines. Original scores and judgements were retained and not aligned; no consensus value was substituted for a disagreement. No meta-analysis was attempted: no outcome was supported by two or more independent comparative studies with the same PICO and a common effect measure ([11] and [13] report one trial at different time points, [12] used a different response definition, and the only DF-specific comparative study [7] was non-randomised and cited in the foreword).

## 3 Results

### 3.1 AGREE II

Domain scores for appraiser 1 were: scope and purpose 83.3% (18/21), stakeholder involvement 61.1% (14/21), rigour of development 45.8% (30/56), clarity of presentation 72.2% (16/21), applicability 41.7% (14/28) and editorial independence 58.3% (9/14); each fraction is obtained/maximum possible, and the percentage is standardised as (obtained − minimum)/(maximum − minimum). Appraiser 2's domain scores were 72.2%, 55.6%, 47.9%, 66.7%, 37.5% and 33.3% for domains 1–6. The three highest-scoring domains were the same for both appraisers (D1>D4>D2), but the lower three were not: editorial independence (D6) ranked fourth for appraiser 1 and last for appraiser 2, a 25.0-percentage-point gap in this two-item domain (domain-rank Spearman ρ=0.83). All other domains differed by ≤11.1 points (Table 1).

Exact agreement was 9/23 items (39.1%). Unweighted κ was 0.258 and linearly weighted κ 0.651, indicating that most disagreements were adjacent on the scale. ICC(2,1) was 0.853 (95%CI 0.663–0.941). The two coefficients are reported together deliberately: κ is sensitive to exact category agreement and is low (Landis–Koch 'fair'), whereas ICC reflects ordering and magnitude and is high. Reading either alone would give a misleading impression. Most divergence was mild and distributed: 12 of 23 items differed by exactly one point and only two by ≥2 points. At item level the mean signed difference was small (≈0.3 points) with no statistically detectable systematic direction (9 positive, 5 negative; sign test p≈0.42), although appraiser 1's domain scores were higher in five of six domains.

Both appraisers scored three items at 1: item 5 (patient and public views), item 14 (update procedure) and item 20 (resource implications). This concordance on the lowest scores is more informative than the overall coefficient, because it identifies the same specific weaknesses from two independent readings. Appraiser 1 rated overall guideline quality 4/7 and recommended use "with modifications"; this global judgement was recorded independently and was not computed from the six domain scores. Disagreements of ≥2 points occurred on item 2 (health problem described, 6 vs 3) and item 22 (funding body's influence, 4 vs 2); original scores were retained and not aligned. The item 2 disagreement concerns the description of the health problem rather than the recommendation itself and did not change domain ordering; item 22 lies in the two-item editorial-independence domain and accounts for that domain's fall from fourth to last place for appraiser 2.

### 3.2 RIGHT

Of 35 subitems, 15 were Yes, 16 were Unclear and 4 were No (Table 2). Items reported included identification as a consensus, publication year, executive summary, target population, users, expert list, methods for evidence-certainty assessment, clear and actionable recommendations with labelled strength and grade, and funding source. In other words, the document is identifiable and readable as a guideline and states what it graded and how strongly. Explicitly not reported were whether patient values and preferences were sought (item 14a), costs and resource implications (14b), the funder's role (18b), and the guideline's limitations (22). These are not minor omissions: item 14a bears on whose preferences informed the recommendations, item 14b on feasibility in resource-constrained settings, and item 22 on the reader's ability to interpret the recommendations in light of the guideline's own constraints. Many Unclear ratings reflected a mention without operational detail — for example subgroup considerations, outcome-selection methods, external review and conflict-of-interest management. Because RIGHT is a reporting instrument, each rating records only whether the information was located in the document; an Unclear or No does not establish that the underlying activity never occurred.

### 3.3 GRADE evidence profile

Across nine outcomes, eight were rated very low certainty and one low (Table 3). The single relatively strong finding was from [11], an open-label randomised trial in painful diabetic neuropathy reporting the composite primary endpoint of ≥50% visual analogue scale relief without neurological deterioration in 75/95 (79%) patients receiving 10-kHz SCS plus conventional medical management versus 5/94 (5%) receiving conventional management alone. Of 216 randomised patients, these were the primary-analysis populations with known 3-month status. The reported difference of 73.6 percentage points (95%CI 64.2–83.0) was computed from the unrounded counts, not by subtracting the rounded percentages; the derived number needed to treat of ≈1.4 applies to this 3-month painful-diabetic-neuropathy endpoint and was not reported by the trial. Because the population was painful diabetic neuropathy, the trial was open-label, and the full text contained no amputation and no DF wound endpoint, certainty for the DF-relevant question was downgraded to low.

For recommendation 1, the adjacent citations [8] (n=17 single-arm), [9] (three-patient case series) and [10] (n=19 single-arm, limb/toe salvage 94.74%) provided no controlled comparison and no randomised limb-salvage trial; both profiled outcomes were very low certainty. The 94.74% salvage figure is reported here as it appears in the source: a single-arm proportion without a comparator baseline, from which an absolute treatment effect cannot be derived. Recommendation 2 had three pain outcomes; two were very low and one low. The contrast between them illustrates how the framework works in practice: the trial with 95 and 94 analysed patients supported a precise effect estimate and retained a low rating, whereas the 36-patient trial, with a serious adverse event and a small sample, and the 24-month follow-up, with crossover and attrition, were rated very low despite comparable or larger reported pain reductions. Recommendations 3, 4, 5 and 6 had no usable body of evidence: the citations attached to recommendation 3 contained no DF wound-healing SCS trial; recommendations 4 and 5 had no numbered citations and were process statements for which GRADE's efficacy-grading framework is not applicable — they are better read as good-practice statements than as very-low-certainty evidence, and their 'very low' labels in the profile denote the absence of a gradeable efficacy body rather than an uncertain but existing one; and two further cited reports addressed PDN pain (including the 24-month follow-up [13]) and health-related quality-of-life outcomes, without a limb-salvage endpoint. One DF-specific controlled study, [7], reported a 12-month total amputation odds ratio of 0.17 (95%CI 0.08–0.37) in 102 SCS versus 104 debridement patients, but allocation was by patient willingness and the study was cited in the foreword rather than attached to the relevant recommendations.

The two appraisers agreed on final certainty for all nine outcomes (9/9; both rated eight very low and one low). Agreement on the domains that produced those ratings was less consistent: exact domain agreement was 100% for inconsistency, 88.9% for publication bias, 66.7% for risk of bias and for imprecision, and 44.4% for indirectness. The two highest rates carry a caveat: agreement on inconsistency (9/9) and on publication bias (8/9) meant agreement that the domain was not assessable, not agreement on a graded judgement, and these rates should not be read as concordance on graded domains. Six of nine outcomes had at least one domain-level difference, although none changed the final certainty. Indirectness was the most frequent point of divergence: for five DF-relevant outcomes (two limb-salvage and three pain outcomes) appraiser 1 recorded 'very serious' where appraiser 2 recorded 'serious', both judging that neuropathy populations cannot answer a diabetic-foot question but differing in the magnitude of downgrading. At least one difference was not purely discretionary: for the 3-month pain outcome, appraiser 1 recorded 'not serious' risk of bias for a trial that was open-label; the source audit identified this as a factual discrepancy, the original judgement was retained, and no consensus value was substituted. The unweighted κ for final certainty was 1.0, but in this near-constant distribution (expected agreement ≈0.80) it follows deterministically from perfect agreement and its bootstrap interval degenerates to [1.00, 1.00]; it is reported only for completeness, and the substantive findings are the exact agreement, the domain-level rates and the disagreement list. Because final certainties were already identical, no adjudication of certainty was performed; original judgements for all six domain-level differences were retained.

### 3.4 Citation–claim verification

Five claims — four quantitative (70% analgesia; 60–80% healing; 40–60% healing at six months; 30–50% amputation reduction) and one qualitative ('SCS reduces amputation') — cited the same source, [6] (Table 4). Its full text, checked against a PDF supplied from the authors' document collection, contained no occurrence of "spinal cord stimulation", "SCS" or "neuromodulation" and none of the five numbers. Numerically similar values existed in open-access full texts ([11] 79%, [12] 59%, [13] 90.1%) but all enrolled painful diabetic neuropathy populations and reported pain rather than limb-salvage outcomes; they were logged as candidate origins, not verified sources. Full text was available for 45 of the 47 references in the supplied folder; the two unavailable items were [6] before the author-supplied PDF was obtained and one German-language guideline, and neither was used as a verified source for an outcome claim.

The methodological significance of the citation result is independent of whether the numbers happen to be approximately correct. A guideline that assigns an evidence grade to a recommendation and then attaches a single review that contains neither the intervention nor the figures has broken the link between claim and support, which is the minimum requirement for a reader to audit the grade. Our verification was deliberately bounded: we checked presence or absence of strings and values in a specified source and did not attempt to reconstruct how the authors arrived at the figures. This distinction matters because an unsupported citation is a documentation failure that can be established from the documents, whereas an incorrect claim would require a different kind of inquiry and judgement. Because such figures can be quoted onward into guideline citations and clinical decisions, the absence of traceable support is clinically consequential even without a finding of falsehood.

## 4 Discussion

This appraisal found a guideline that reports its scope and recommendations reasonably clearly but whose methodological rigour, applicability and evidence–recommendation linkage are weak. The AGREE II pattern — high scope and clarity, low applicability and rigour — has been reported in other guideline-appraisal studies and suggests that the main improvement opportunity lies in how evidence is generated, linked and updated rather than in how recommendations are written. The agreement between two independent appraisers on the three highest-scoring domains and on the location of the lowest-scored items, despite low exact item agreement (κ=0.258), indicates that the overall judgement is robust in its broad outline; it was not uniform across all six domains, because editorial independence ranked fourth for one appraiser and last for the other. The practical reading is therefore not that the appraisers agreed on every detail, but that they converged on the main strengths and on the key weaknesses.

Three findings merit emphasis in sequence. First, the RIGHT results show that the document contains the structural elements readers expect — a stated purpose, a target population, named experts, labelled recommendations — while omitting the contextual elements that would allow those recommendations to be interpreted, in particular patient preferences, resource implications and the guideline's own limitations. Second, the AGREE II results locate the weakness in process rather than presentation: the items that both appraisers scored at 1 concern public involvement, updating and resource consequences, all of which require prospective planning rather than editorial polishing. Third, the GRADE reconstruction shows that the evidence cited for the DF questions is largely from a different population with different endpoints, and that several recommendations carry no citable evidence body at all. These three layers are mutually reinforcing rather than redundant.

The GRADE reconstruction identified indirectness as the cross-cutting problem. The consensus used a USPSTF-style net-benefit and recommendation-strength framework (grades high/moderate/low; strengths A/B/C/D/I), which is distinct from GRADE; our profiles are an external retrospective reconstruction, not a re-grading on the same scale. The consensus addresses diabetic foot ulceration, ischaemia and limb salvage, whereas the randomised evidence it relies on enrolled patients with painful diabetic neuropathy and measured pain. Pain relief in neuropathy is a legitimate clinical outcome, but it cannot substitute for wound healing or amputation avoidance in a DF population. Four recommendations had no usable evidence body at all, and two of those were process statements for which GRADE's efficacy-rating framework is not the appropriate tool; they are better described as good-practice statements. The [7] cohort is the only DF-specific controlled study found, and its non-random allocation and placement in the foreword limit its use as support for the graded recommendations.

The citation check is the most consequential factual finding. Four quantitative numbers and one qualitative claim all traced to a single narrative review that, on full-text inspection, discusses neither SCS nor those numbers. We deliberately report this as a source–claim mismatch rather than as misconduct: the figures may derive from the authors' synthesis or another source, and determining whether a statement is false belongs to formal academic review. Nevertheless, for a guideline that assigns 'high' net-benefit grades to recommendations while printing these headline efficacy numbers in its foreword and efficacy-assessment section against a single review that contains neither the intervention nor the figures, the absence of traceable support breaks the evidence–recommendation link and is a methodological concern in its own right.

Several limitations constrain interpretation. First, this is an appraisal of a single guideline, so generalisation to other SCS guidelines is not warranted; the results describe this document and should not be read as a statement about the field as a whole. Second, the GRADE profiles are a retrospective reconstruction from the consensus' own citations and may have missed relevant studies outside those references; a de novo systematic review with a registered search and independent screening would be needed to establish the complete evidence base. Reconstructing from cited literature also means that a citation placed in the foreword rather than beside a recommendation was scored according to that placement, which is faithful to the document but may understate evidence the authors considered elsewhere. Third, RIGHT was applied by one reviewer, so no RIGHT inter-rater agreement is available; RIGHT is a reporting checklist and this does not affect the certainty judgements. GRADE was independently duplicated, but both appraisers worked from the same set of verified source facts, so agreement reflects consistency of judgement given those facts, not independence of source retrieval. Fourth, three of the prioritised citations remained full-text closed at initial checking, although the supplied folder later resolved two of them; bibliographic-level facts are labelled as such. Fifth, the AGREE II global quality rating and use recommendation were provided by one appraiser only, so no agreement statistic is available for that component. Sixth, two appraisers meet the AGREE II minimum but the instrument preferably uses four, and all coefficients derive from a small number of items or outcomes from one document; the GRADE κ is uninformative in a near-constant distribution (bootstrap interval [1.00, 1.00]). These coefficients should be read alongside their item- and domain-level disagreement lists, not as precise estimates.

The practical implication is concrete and actionable at three levels. For guideline developers, each graded recommendation should be attached to a clearly identified, population- and endpoint-concordant evidence source, and process statements should be labelled as good practice rather than given efficacy grades. For clinicians, three points follow: the headline figures of 70%, 60–80% and 30–50% should not be quoted as DF-specific SCS efficacy because their sole cited source does not contain them; the positive SCS evidence comes from painful diabetic neuropathy and is not evidence of limb salvage; and no randomised DF limb-salvage trial was identified among the consensus' own references. For health-system decision-makers, a traceable evidence–recommendation link is a precondition for using a consensus in technology-adoption and coverage decisions, and an auditable method such as ours can be reapplied when a guideline is revised. Low certainty does not mean SCS is ineffective; it means the cited evidence does not justify a high-certainty label for diabetic-foot outcomes. We recommend that the consensus be revised with explicit PICO-linked citation mapping and updated using DF-specific comparative research.

## 5 Conclusion

In this cross-sectional methodological appraisal of a 2026 Chinese expert consensus on SCS for diabetic foot, reporting of scope and recommendations was moderately good, but rigour, applicability and evidence linkage were weak. Eight of nine profiled outcomes had very low certainty, four recommendations lacked a usable evidence body, and five claims (four quantitative, one qualitative) were not supported by their sole cited source. These findings call for methodological revision of the consensus and for DF-specific comparative research; they do not establish that SCS is ineffective.
