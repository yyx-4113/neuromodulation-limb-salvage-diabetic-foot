Dear Editors,

We are submitting our manuscript entitled "Methodological appraisal of a Chinese expert consensus on spinal cord stimulation for diabetic foot" for consideration as a Research article in *BMC Health Services Research*.

**Why this manuscript fits the journal.** This study is not a clinical trial of spinal cord stimulation and does not claim to establish treatment efficacy. It is a cross-sectional methodological appraisal of one published clinical practice guideline: we evaluated how the guideline was developed and reported, whether its self-assigned evidence grades are supported by the sources it cites, and whether the certainty of evidence for each outcome justifies those grades. This question sits squarely within health-system quality and safety, and aligns with the journal's stated policy that editorial decisions are based on scientific validity rather than the perceived interest or potential impact of a study. We believe the findings will be of interest to readers concerned with guideline trustworthiness, evidence-to-recommendation linkage, and the reliability of consensus documents in a rapidly adopted intervention.

**What we found.** Applying three complementary instruments to the 2026 Chinese expert consensus, we observed a consistent pattern: scope and presentation were reported reasonably clearly, while rigour, applicability and evidence linkage were weak. AGREE II domain scores ranged from 41.7% to 83.3% for appraiser 1 and from 33.3% to 72.2% for appraiser 2; the three highest-scoring domains matched but editorial independence ranked fourth and last respectively. Exact item agreement was 39.1% (κ 0.258, weighted κ 0.651, ICC(2,1) 0.853, 95% CI 0.663–0.941). RIGHT classified 15 subitems as Yes, 16 as Unclear and 4 as No. In the GRADE reconstruction, eight of nine outcomes were rated very low certainty and one low, and four of six recommendations had no usable body of evidence; the cross-cutting problem was indirectness, because the randomised evidence enrolled patients with painful diabetic neuropathy and measured pain rather than diabetic-foot wound or limb-salvage outcomes. Two independent appraisers reached identical final certainty ratings for all nine outcomes. Finally, four quantitative claims and one qualitative claim in the consensus all cited a single reference whose full text contained neither spinal cord stimulation nor any of the claimed numbers. We report this as a source–claim mismatch established from the documents, not as a determination about intent.

**Methodological strengths.** The three instruments were used on their own terms rather than collapsed into a single score. AGREE II and GRADE were each applied by two independent appraisers working from masked materials; RIGHT, a reporting checklist, was applied by one reviewer and is reported as such. We verified cited sources at full-text level where obtainable and labelled abstract-only or bibliographic-level facts explicitly. All judgements and agreement statistics were computed from deposited CSV files by reproducible scripts, and original ratings were retained rather than aligned. We deliberately framed the study as an appraisal of a guideline, not a systematic review of efficacy, and did not pre-specify that the consensus was wrong.

**Ethics and data.** This study evaluated a published guideline and its cited literature and involved no human participants, human samples or identifiable data; institutional review board approval was therefore not applicable. The appraisal dataset, scoring templates, extracted citation facts, scripts and a SHA-256 MANIFEST are available in a named public repository, with the repository URL and release tag given in the manuscript Data Availability statement, and an archival copy at doi:10.5281/zenodo.24461453.
